What Lives Where

Ce contenu n’est pas encore disponible dans votre langue.

Everything jinflow holds sits in one of three places. Which place a thing lives in decides three practical questions: can it be regenerated, does it travel with you, and who owns it. If you are evaluating jinflow — as a customer, or as a partner shipping your own analytics on it — this is the page that answers what do I actually get, and what can I take away.

The three strata

Section titled “The three strata”
holdsif you lost it
The AFS — the frameworkyour signals, theses, verdicts, entities, expert knowledgeit is the source. Kept in git, so it is recoverable the way any source repository is
The KLS — the knowledge storeevery built result: the tables, the findings, the verdicts as computedrebuild it. jin make regenerates it from the AFS and your data
The OPS — the operational storenotes people wrote, bookmarks, what has been read, who may do whatrestore it from backup. Nothing regenerates a note somebody typed

That last row is the one worth pausing on. A 450 MB knowledge store is disposable — delete it and one command brings it back. A 2 MB operational store is not. This inverts the intuition that big things are precious, and it is why jinflow treats them under completely different rules: the KLS is output, the OPS is record.

Why they are separate

Section titled “Why they are separate”

Because they answer to different masters. The AFS is authored — a person decides what a signal means. The KLS is derived — no one decides anything about it; it is what the AFS says applied to the data you have. The OPS is lived — it accumulates as people use the system, and nobody could reconstruct it from first principles even in theory.

Mixing them looks convenient and costs you the moment something goes wrong. If notes lived in the KLS, every rebuild would destroy them. If the framework lived in the operational store, you could not review a change before it took effect.

Artifacts and citizens

Section titled “Artifacts and citizens”

The AFS holds artifacts. The OPS holds citizens. They are different words because they behave differently, not because we like vocabulary.

An artifact is authored, versioned in git, and travels: every built KLS carries a copy of the exact framework that produced it, so a result can always be traced to the declaration behind it.

A citizen is operational. It is never regenerated — only restored from backup — and it does not travel: a KLS you send someone carries no notes, no bookmarks, and no people.

That last point is deliberate and worth stating plainly: a knowledge store carries data and analytics, never your colleagues. Identities and permissions live in the operational store, not in the artifact you hand to someone.

Two kinds of artifact

Section titled “Two kinds of artifact”

Artifacts divide again, and the line matters for anyone building on jinflow.

Business artifacts declare what the analysis is:

signals · perspectives · theses · verdicts · entities · Subject Matter · dossiers · observations · contributing factors · interventions · suggestions · inquiry

Application artifacts declare how it is shown:

maps · panels · tiles · pages · lenses · tours

The distinction is not cosmetic. A business artifact can change your numbers; an application artifact cannot. Recolouring a map, adding a panel, rearranging a page — none of that can alter a single finding. Changing a signal’s threshold changes what the system reports.

That gives you a review rule for free: a change touching only application artifacts needs no rebuild and can be reviewed for taste. A change touching business artifacts changes results and deserves the scrutiny you would give a change to any calculation.

It also draws an ownership line. A partner shipping a domain pack ships business artifacts — that is the expertise. Application artifacts are far more often the product’s own.

What is actually in an AFS

Section titled “What is actually in an AFS”

A real one, so this is concrete rather than aspirational. Every tracked path is something a person wrote:

the business artifacts · the application artifacts · the dbt models and macros that shape your data · contracts · CSV schemas · the glossary · calibration · lineage · the tenant’s own configuration and branding

No compiled output is tracked. Not one generated model, registry or report. What the engine writes, the AFS excludes — and it says so in one place, its own .gitignore:

Two tracked files are machine-written, deliberately. .pack-sync.json is a checksum of every file a pack seeded, so an upgrade can tell “you edited this” from “the pack changed this”; .pack-init.yml records what the tenant was born from. Both are provenance rather than product — and provenance is useless if it does not travel with the thing it describes.

So the rule is not quite everything here is authored. It is:

Everything in the AFS is either authored, or a record of where the authored things came from. Nothing derived from your data lives here.

A useful test when adding something new: would a build write this? If yes it does not belong — not for tidiness, but because the AFS is what a build reads. Anything a build writes into it becomes an input to the next build, and that is a loop.

The test holds cleanly for a single pipeline. In a multi-stage arrangement one stage’s output is the next stage’s input, so “written by a build” stops meaning “derived and disposable” — it means “produced here, consumed there”. Those intermediates still are not AFS artifacts; the AFS merely names them, which is why a binding that points at something nothing produces is refused before a build starts.

Things move — but only when someone moves them

Section titled “Things move — but only when someone moves them”

The three strata are not sealed. Work is promoted across them, and every promotion is a deliberate gesture rather than a drift.

Read left to right, that is the path from someone noticed something to the framework now checks for it: brush a chart, keep the selection, promote it to a signal, and it runs on every build from then on.

This is what keeps “authored” a meaningful word even though the AFS gains things that began life elsewhere. Nothing migrates on its own. A draft stays a draft until published; a selection stays a selection until promoted. The stratum a thing sits in always reflects a decision somebody made, which is why you can review the framework and trust that it is the whole of it.

What a policy is

Section titled “What a policy is”

A tenant’s access policy is an operational citizen with three deliberately separate facets, because they carry different stakes:

  • writable — may people save notes and bookmarks here?
  • showcase — does it appear as a demonstration?
  • visibility — is it reachable without signing in?

They are kept apart so that “let people take notes” is never accidentally the same decision as “let the public read this.”

Two properties are worth knowing because they are what make the mechanism trustworthy. Absent means default — a tenant with no policy row simply follows its declared kind, so nothing needs a migration and nothing silently diverges. And the defaults are the safe end of every facet: private, read-only, not a showcase. A policy written carelessly can only ever be more closed than intended, never more open.

What this means if you are a partner

Section titled “What this means if you are a partner”

Your framework is yours, and it is a git repository. Not an export, not a proprietary bundle — the same clone your team already knows how to work with.

You can leave with it. The AFS travels as a git clone; every KLS carries the framework that built it. Ending a relationship does not strand your analytics inside somebody’s platform.

You ship business artifacts. The engine provides the compilers, the store, JinDesk and the contracts. Your pack declares the analysis. The seam between those is a published contract rather than a private arrangement.

And your customers’ operational data is not yours or ours to move. Notes, bookmarks and permissions live per-tenant, in a store that is copied out only by a deliberate act.

How each one lives

Section titled “How each one lives”

The three behave differently over time, and the differences are the point.

A business artifact

Section titled “A business artifact”

Authored once, then compiled on every build. It is the only category that can change what your numbers say.

Two loops matter. A pack seeds a tenant and the tenant then owns its copy — your edits are yours, and a pack upgrade is something you pull deliberately, not something that happens to you. And a change worth sharing can travel back up to the pack.

An application artifact

Section titled “An application artifact”

The same authoring path, and then it stops short of the KLS — because it has nothing to say about results.

Nothing here recomputes a finding. A map’s tiles ride inside the KLS so they travel with it — which is why a map’s own geometry works with no network — but the declaration is read at render time.

A citizen

Section titled “A citizen”

Created by people using the system, and never regenerated by anything.

The dashed edge is the whole category. There is no arrow from anywhere into a citizen except a person or a restore — which is why the backup is the only thing standing between you and losing it.

Versioning is partly built: the storage keeps every version of an authored citizen, and the full verb set — undelete, purge — is still being landed.

The KLS, for contrast

Section titled “The KLS, for contrast”

The arrow back to the AFS is the point: losing a KLS costs you a rebuild, not a fact. Every one of them carries a copy of the framework that produced it, so a result can always be traced to the declaration behind it.

In one line each

Section titled “In one line each”
  • AFS — what you declared. Git. Travels. Yours.
  • KLS — what that produced. Rebuildable. Disposable.
  • OPS — what happened since. Backed up, never regenerated. Stays put.
