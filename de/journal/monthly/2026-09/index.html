September 2026 — The month one word stopped covering two things

Dieser Inhalt ist noch nicht in deiner Sprache verfügbar.

1 Sep – 30 Sep 2026 · 177 commits · 12 new design papers · 0.63.x → 0.64.x

August forbade the system from guessing when it did not know something. September found the harder version of that problem: not an absent declaration but a present one that was answering two questions at once. A single name, sitting over two states that needed opposite handling, returning one answer and looking entirely correct while doing it.

The team coined the phrase for it mid-month — the same two-things-one-word shape — and by October it had been written up as a standing rule: split, never widen. It belongs to September because this is the month it stopped being a series of coincidences. Eleven of the month’s twelve design papers are literally that form, and their titles say so: a field wearing a canonical name; what kind: must mean, and why it currently means nothing; one hospital’s geography written into pack law; what is pack, what is tenant, and what was pack; a Selection lights its related rows; what a model does when the source is silent; the knowledge store must survive its own filename; which verb frees them.

Here is the month in one table, because the repetition is the argument:

one wordthe two states it hid
a probe’s silencelooked and found nothing vs never looked
syncedan object exists vs the copy is as new as the original
an empty resultevaluated clean vs abstained
is_billable = truethe measurement vs the assumption
kind:what a rebuild needs vs who authored it
”no common ancestor”newly added by the pack vs both sides created it
a routing entrythe address vs the exposure
the fleet’s one homeper-installation vs spans-installations
a clone’s certificateits own birth vs its parent’s
a department codea department vs a storage location
a lit rowselected vs related to something selected
refusedby design vs could not

Even the headline figure above is an instance. 177 commits, of which 68 are correspondence — a count that quietly covers two different activities, and whose code half is 109.

The probes were lying

Section titled “The probes were lying”

The month opened with Mig asking whether the smoke tests still meant anything.

The browser canary had not run in 103 days. Its trigger was a cloud deploy workflow that had also last run that same day in May, deployment having moved to a laptop in between — a canary whose trigger depends on who deployed is the wrong shape once a laptop is a deploy path. The ship’s own “smoke tests” stage counted knowledge-store files on the local disk, before deploying anything, and printed Smoke tests passed.

Re-pointed at a schedule on the Monday, it ran zero tests and reported neutral on the Tuesday, because its condition keyed on the deploy event that no longer fired. Then the tenant matrix turned out to have collapsed to a single leg: I verified a matrix by using the one trigger that disables it. Then ten of thirty-six tests had never been able to run at all, while the summary said twenty-four passed and nothing said a third of the suite had sat out.

Behind those, four more of the same shape. The version-drift probe compared served versions against a gitignored placeholder, so it abstained on every comparison — and abstaining renders as nothing to report, which is indistinguishable from current. The health endpoint could not see the deployed version at all. The journal publication check compared the working tree against the working tree, the one surface where the answer is always yes. A smoke script had been dead since early August on an empty-array expansion under the shell the project documents.

What makes this a movement rather than a tidy-up is what the revived probes immediately found: a flat route that never established its tenant context, so every query failed and a swallowed exception told authors this column is not typed while the table held 460 rows of types. And on production, three components claiming the same screen corner, where a person simply could not click one of them.

Two silences that were clearing real alarms

Section titled “Two silences that were clearing real alarms”

Two findings were not reporting-layer problems. They were changing answers.

The Bell cleared alarms nobody had checked. Auto-clearing rested on an inference — the source ran, looked, and found nothing — which only holds when the source could look. A lean build purges the intermediate tables at finalisation, so the next incremental build handed the validity source no tables at all: not clean data, no data. It returned an empty list, indistinguishable from an all-clear, and the aggregator cleared two rings covering roughly 2,800 genuinely invalid rows. A crashing plugin cleared its rings the same way. A source now has three outcomes rather than two — rings, evaluated-empty, or abstained — and the aggregator’s rule became positive: a ring clears only when its own source ran to completion this build. Surfaced on the way, and worse in its way: the diagnostic source had never produced a ring in its life, because it ran before the table it reads was built.

And a knowledge store could not survive its own filename. The engine derives a catalog name from the file name, and the build tool emits fully-qualified view definitions, so the filename is baked into the stored SQL of every view — and views re-bind at query time. Rename it, which is all a snapshot is, and every view names a catalog that no longer exists. Measured: all seventeen stores on the machine carried filename-bound views, and in the snapshots they were already dead. The lean pass had known, and had worked around it rather than removing it. Normalisation now runs at the end of every build and identifies the qualifier structurally, so a file renamed long ago repairs too — while repairing a snapshot stays a deliberate script, because it mutates something the product calls immutable.

A pack may carry the mechanism and the question, never one customer’s answer

Section titled “A pack may carry the mechanism and the question, never one customer’s answer”

Two findings in the same week, in the same lane, with the same shape: a value that belonged to one tenant had been written into law for all of them.

A pack model labelled every department by code prefix — one digit for one town, another for a second, a letter for the pharmacy. On the synthetic demo tenant, whose codes all begin with that first digit, all 498 departments carried a Valais town’s name, on a publicly served tenant. Complete and wrong, so no coverage check ever complained. Mig challenged the premise — that the letter prefix was a source-system convention — and was right: the delivery carries numeric codes only, and every letter sits in a per-site file, because that hospital takes its medication movements as two separate exports. The letter was never a convention. It was which file the row came from.

The rule: a tenant’s organisational convention is declared by the tenant. Pack SQL may know that a site exists; it may not know what it is called. And undeclared means NULL — not “Other”, which is “I don’t know” in a costume, sorting and filtering alongside real sites as though it were one.

Its sibling: on the other hospital tenant, every material read as billable — 12,331 of 12,331 — from one literal string in an extractor, with 1,632 signal findings resting on it and nothing anywhere saying so. The line below it returned the same value on both branches of a conditional: a constant wearing the shape of logic. Two problems and one judgement call. The extractor stops inventing bytes at the trust boundary, which the zero-transform rule already settled. What a model does with genuinely unknown billability became a declaration in the configuration cascade, defaulting to exclusion — absent means refuse — with the data carrying the measurement and the policy side by side, so the assumption never overwrites the fact.

The join became a product surface, and a live data-loss bug

Section titled “The join became a product surface, and a live data-loss bug”

Mig’s requirement arrived as commerce rather than as a bug report: we have to keep the join. One reason: it will be a paid service to the customer to frequently release improved packs. The customer must have the chance to understand which elements in his own tenants are affected.

That sent someone to read the pack-to-tenant update path properly, and it was losing data. Two files a sister had authored herself were listed under “Updated files” — clean, unflagged. Applying it would have replaced her work and reported success. A third file, which happened to have a common ancestor, was correctly flagged as a conflict with both sides’ history.

The cause was one line and a comment describing a case other than the one its code was handling. No common ancestor was being treated as the pack just added this — but by that line the code had already established that the tenant has the file and the contents differ, so it can only mean both sides created the path independently. That now routes into the both-sides-changed path: identical content adopts silently, differing content is a conflict and never an overwrite, because absent an ancestor we cannot know whether the tenant’s version is customisation, and “no” is the one guess that destroys their work.

A conflict is not a resting place either, so a second verb ended it by recording the pack’s current content as the base. Applied to one pack’s tenants: forty files identical, and exactly the two divergent ones were the two she had authored, her wording verified surviving. The author’s own summary of the day is the best sentence in the month: silent overwrite, then permanent conflict, then a clean merge that keeps them — the middle state was necessary, but it is not a resting place. And the argument for the timing: adoption is lossless precisely while the copies still agree.

One number reframes the whole update path. Across nine live tenants, 772 seeded files have drifted against 750 untouched. “The tenant edited a seeded file” is the common case, not the edge case.

The fleet got a seam, and the record was made to catch up

Section titled “The fleet got a seam, and the record was made to catch up”

Mig studied the fleet deck mid-month and ruled: presences, not tenants, are what gets registered — a tenant is an identity, only a presence has a place. Three invented words were replaced by Cohort, Organisation and Site. One store per installation. No fleet identifier.

The routing citizen narrowed from five fields to two, and the reasoning generalises past routing: a security bit with several homes eventually fails open, because somebody edits the copy they know about. The test it leaves behind is reusable — if the same tenant is served by two hosts, must the answer be the same? Yes, the tenant owns it; no, the host owns it; routing owns neither. The decisive evidence was one row of the live host file, where two hosts serve no tenant at all and disagree about an auth flag.

Then the seam itself, and the best correction of the month. It was first ruled as the store holds what dies when the deployment dies — and replaced within minutes by a better reason, found by the sister whose own paper the ruling displaced: the store is per-installation, the declared map spans installations, the seam is a scope boundary, and a fact belongs to whichever scope it is true at. She had re-read her own specification and found its premise had dissolved rather than been contradicted, which is a different and more useful thing to be able to say.

The record then had to catch up, and had been charging interest: three decks, two of them at public addresses, still called a settled question “the top open item” two days after it was ruled, and one contradicted itself on the same public page.

One supercommand, held at the gate for eight days

Section titled “One supercommand, held at the gate for eight days”

Mig asked for one supercommand, everything to the cloud and back — three times in five days, believing it already existed. The constraint that shaped it is that an operator has only the binary, never the project’s recipes, so the aggregate had to be a verb rather than a recipe.

The specification landed on the 20th and not a line was written for eight days while two cross-lane reviews ran. Each ruling removed something. The up-leg must delegate to the existing publish verb rather than re-compose its three steps, because those three are that verb, and sequencing them twice drifts the day it gains a fourth. A publication flag was refused outright, verified on a real dry run: the default audience carries the blacklist that keeps the private hospital tenant out of a shared bucket, and the alternative has no blacklist at all — an operator-facing converge verb is exactly where someone reaches for that flag. Mig corrected the stage ordering and an engineer with it: the framework must come down before the build, or the build runs on a stale framework.

And the fourth review added the stage nobody had asked for. Nothing converged the operational store’s baked citizens — the capability, pass, role and identity definitions. The publish verb guarantees the store exists; the union verb handles the event-keyed rows; nobody handled baked. Measured on two tenants: current identity trees locally, cloud trees months behind, and the Bell invisible and unresolvable for every signed-in user, because the desk resolves identity from the cloud tree first. Shipping did not fix it and could not. The verdict is the sentence the whole review existed to produce: as specced, this would make that permanent and fleet-wide — every stage passing, the operator told everything had converged.

Built on the 28th: five stages, no new transport, each stage an existing verb run as a subprocess so it keeps its own refusals and exit code, and the plan prints each stage’s direction, so the honest asymmetry — the knowledge store goes up only, the operational store converges both ways — is said out loud on every run.

The outward turn

Section titled “The outward turn”

Downstream of a deep codebase review on the 7th — 108 agents across eleven subsystem slices, 29 of 32 findings confirmed: seven critical, fifteen security, held local for its live-exploit detail — the month turned outward for a week.

There was no roadmap. Five partial ones, no dates, and a missing-for-production document still describing the shipped cloud concept as future work, so the doc predates its own subject. Now there is one, with a spine — by year-end, someone who is not Mig runs a jinflow tenant on their own data, and nothing about that is dangerous — four phases, three honesty markers including an explicit bet, and a done-when clause naming evidence on every item.

Mig’s call to be fully open was taken. The deck states zero customers, 29 findings, seven critical, and a scheduled lockout, on the argument that a pre-customer product with no findings has not been looked at, while one with findings and a repayment date has. It also names the bottleneck out loud, because the repository shows it either way: decision latency, not build throughput — one unruled question held a finished component for weeks while 423 commits landed around it.

And the decks turned out to be safe by accident. A sister grepped the public fleet deck for every client, organisation and tenant name and found nothing — but her point was the status of that cleanliness: the deck declared nothing, so its safety was a property nobody was checking, at an address anyone can reach. Two refusals now stand in the publishing script — a deck must declare itself public, absent being a refusal rather than a default, and must not name a private tenant. Her keeper: a deck is publishable because it argues about shapes rather than examples.

Why splitting a word is slower than adding one

Section titled “Why splitting a word is slower than adding one”

The figures look like a collapse. July: 1,421 commits, 116 design papers. August: 926 and 55. September: 177 and 12 — an eighth of July’s volume.

It is not an artifact, and three independent counts fall together. Lines added dropped 157,702 → 81,227 → 18,018. The pack repositories fell 43 → 12 and the tenant frameworks 244 → 55, both harder in proportion. Nothing is hiding on unmerged branches.

But it is not a slowdown either, and the mix says what it is. fix: overtook feat: for the first time in the project’s life — 32 against 22, where features had been the plurality in July (431) and August (189). The note: lane closed entirely: zero all month, down from 102 in July, the shared log having moved wholesale into the mailboxes. Commits got bigger as they got rarer — 126, then 137, then 165 lines each. And 45 per cent of September’s commit bodies name Mig, up from 19 per cent in July.

That last figure is the explanation. The month’s three largest findings were each triggered by a sentence from him rather than by a test: we have to keep the join found a live data-loss bug; we have to settle this properly before we can think about the workstation found a classification field wrong for 41 per cent of entities; are those two tenants up to date in the cloud? found all eleven syncable tenants stale while every one reported synced.

Which is the real difference. August’s output was artifacts — stores, verbs, surfaces, flagships. September’s dominant output was distinctions, and a distinction arrives as a third enum value, a line in a paper, and a ruling. None of those move a commit counter very much. Adding a word is fast and can be done alone. Splitting one requires finding both meanings, establishing that they need opposite handling, deciding who owns each side, and getting somebody to rule — and the eight days that specification sat untouched at the gate are what that costs.

August stopped the system guessing when it did not know. September found the cases where it did know, had a word for it, and the word was carrying two answers at once. The month’s whole yield is the instruction that follows: split, never widen — and unknown is never fine.

The month in figures

Section titled “The month in figures”
  • 177 commits — an eighth of July’s 1,421, and 68 of them are correspondence; the code half is 109. The figure itself conflates two things, which is the month in miniature.
  • 12 new design papers, against August’s 55 — and eleven of the twelve are the split of a conflated word: the canonical-name field, the entity kinds, the site convention, the pack-tenant join, the fleet register, related rows, the declared assumption, filename portability, the River rename, which verb frees a stranded note, one operator verb.
  • 13 releases, 0.63.39 → 0.64.9 — the ISO weeks W36–W40.
  • fix: overtook feat: for the first time — 32 to 22.
  • Zero note: commits and zero refactor: commits all month.
  • 29 findings confirmed by the deep review of the 7th, 7 critical, held local — the bill October’s lockout would pay.
