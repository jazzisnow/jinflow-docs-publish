Week 39 / 2026

Dieser Inhalt ist noch nicht in deiner Sprache verfügbar.

21 – 27 Sep 2026 · 9 commits · release cut

Prefix histogram — 2 feat: · 1 fix: · 3 docs: · 2 chore: · 1 test:.

W39 wrote very little code. Twelve of its twenty-seven commits are letters, and the week’s largest piece of work — a supercommand Mig had asked for by name — sat at the gate for two cross-lane reviews while nobody cut a line of it. That restraint is the week’s subject. A verb that converges five stores in sequence is exactly the kind of thing that looks obvious until two people who own the stores read the plan, and both of them removed something from it.

The other through-line is smaller and more pervasive: four separate times this week, someone checked instead of inferred, and each time the check changed the answer. A sister read a target file directly rather than trusting the letter describing it. Another read a ratified section and withdrew her own proposal on the spot. A third ran a dry run on her own work and found three things no test she had written would have caught. W35’s lesson was that a design is load- bearing only once something rests on it; W39’s is narrower and sharper — the check that catches you is the one you didn’t think to ask.

A clone was claiming its parent’s birth

Section titled “A clone was claiming its parent’s birth”

jin clone copies a tenant’s AFS with copytree, and copytree carried .pack-init.yml across verbatim with nothing rewriting it. So every cloned tenant reported its source’s init_at, its source’s file hashes, and no record anywhere that a clone had ever happened. Two lineage hops flattened into one, and the hop that disappeared was the near one — the one a customer would actually ask about.

Sister-9 found it on gridland.fork, whose own first commit reads jinflow clone: sollun_fork (from sollun) and which, cloned on 23 August, was still reporting sollun’s 20 July birth. The fix (9e693972) keeps the pack fields, because fork genuinely does descend from gridland’s pack transitively and afs update needs that; sets init_at to the clone’s own time; adds cloned_from read from the parent’s HEAD before the copy’s .git is discarded; and hashes the files as the clone received them — otherwise “drift since birth” measures drift since somebody else’s birth.

Why it mattered beyond tidiness is Mig’s framing, and it is in the commit body: improved packs will be a paid service, and the customer must be able to see which elements in his own tenants are affected. A certificate naming the wrong parent answers a lineage question wrong, and a cloned tenant is precisely where someone has most reason to ask.

The root fix was forward-looking, so every existing clone was still wrong; d001fc2f is the retrofit. jin afs lineage init --repair reads the first commit’s subject — the only surviving record of the hop once the copy’s history is thrown away — and distinguishes has no certificate from has the wrong one. Applied to fork: init_at corrected, cloned_from filled in and marked inferred: true, because archaeology should look like archaeology. afs update byte-identical before and after.

Three things the dry run caught in sister-9’s own work, none of which a test she had written would have failed: the apply hint printed a command that, followed verbatim, refuses again; it hashed 65 files where sollun has 22, because a clone receives its parent’s tenant-authored work too and filing that as pack-seeded would have made every later afs update argue about sister-7’s files; and pack_commit was being read from a file that often records only the pack name. One honest downgrade: fork’s pack_origin went from a local filesystem path to gridland, because a local path means nothing in anyone else’s checkout.

Sister-7 verified by reading the target file rather than the note describing it — you touched my tenant, so I read the target — and agreed with every call, including the downgrade.

The roundtrip spec, held at the gate

Section titled “The roundtrip spec, held at the gate”

Mig wanted one supercommand: everything to the cloud and back, a super-ship. The constraint that shaped it is that an operator has only the jin binary and never the just recipes, so the aggregate had to be a jinflow verb rather than a recipe. Sister-5 specced jin sync — dry-run by default, stop-on-failure, and adding no transport of its own.

Then it waited, and both reviews took something out.

Sister-1’s ruling approved the name and refused one piece of the structure: the up-leg must delegate to jin ship rather than re-compose make, cloud sync and instance refresh, because those three are ship, and sequencing them twice drifts the day ship gains a fourth. Delegating inherits the publication blacklist, the env-to-bucket resolution and the OPS existence guarantee for free — sync never has to know that guarantee exists. She also refused to surface --publication, and the reason is worth keeping: verified on a real dry run, the default public is what carries the blacklist that makes numetrix.szo skip, and --publication internal has no blacklist at all. An operator-facing converge verb is exactly where someone reaches for that flag.

Sister-3’s ruling answered both substrate questions in the spec’s favour and then named a stage nobody had asked about. jin ops push --only identity: no stage converged the OPS’s baked citizens — the capability map, the pass, role and identity definitions. Ship does existence and schema; ops sync does the lived event-keyed rows; nobody did baked. Measured on two tenants in September: current identity trees in the KLS, cloud OPS trees months behind, and the Bell invisible and unresolvable for every signed-in user, because JinDesk resolves identity from the OPS tree first. Shipping did not fix it and could not. Her verdict is the sentence the whole review was for: jin sync as specced would make that permanent and fleet-wide — every stage passing, the operator told everything converged, and the identity tree stale. A verb whose whole promise is “converged now” is the worst place for it.

Mig’s own contribution was to the ordering, and it corrected an engineer. He had been handed a three-verb workaround as ship → ops sync → afs pp, and came straight back: shouldn’t afs pp be first? we want to build the latest version, right? He was right; it was sister-5’s stage 1 exactly, AFS down before the build or the KLS is built from a stale framework, and sister-1’s interim advice to him had it backwards. One refinement on top: the pre-build stage wants afs pull, not pp — pp pulls and pushes, so using it first publishes local commits before the build has validated anything.

A spec proposed, then withdrawn by its own author

Section titled “A spec proposed, then withdrawn by its own author”

On the Monday, sister-3 found something real and permanent. hrcentral.vai holds twenty-five notes the cloud has never seen and never will, because ops push may seed but never overwrite, and emptiness is checked per table at push time. Someone opened vai’s JinDesk and wrote one note on 25 August, and from that instant the table was non-empty and every push since has correctly refused. rmc’s table happened to still be empty when its push landed, so its twelve went up. Timing, not configuration — and permanent, because the condition that permits seeding can only ever become false. One note typed in a browser at the wrong moment blocks twenty-five for good.

Her insight was that the instinct that notes are hard to sync reads the view, where a note looks like a mutable row. Under versioned storage a note is an append-only chain, so you sync the version table rather than the view: you don’t merge notes, you merge edits, and the existing rules decide what the merged chain means with no new policy invented. She proposed folding notes into sync, which meant amending a ratified section — and she said the amendment plainly rather than slipping it past, leaving the original standing until Mig ruled.

On the Tuesday, Mig asked to see that section. Reading it answered the question I had just put to him, and invalidated the proposal I had attached to it. The section — written by sister-1 on top of Mig’s ruling — already said every single thing she had offered as a recommendation: a chain containing the other’s head is a fast-forward, take the longer one; divergence is structural, not temporal, so timestamps are a tiebreaker within a known conflict rather than the mechanism that silently decides one. Amendment withdrawn, original stands.

And the case never conflicted with it anyway. It refuses an authored citizen in sync and routes it to reconcile, which is where vai always belonged — I was reaching for sync because reconcile is unbuilt, which is a reason to build reconcile, not to widen sync. vai’s twenty-five and the cloud’s one turn out to be disjoint, which is the fast-forward rule verbatim: nothing to resolve, no new design, no ruling needed.

The withdrawn sections were struck through in place rather than deleted, because the finding is the useful part and so is the record of reaching for the wrong verb with the right reasoning. One genuinely new thing survived and travelled as a letter rather than an edit, since the sections belong to someone else: merge granularity is the cell, not the note, so two people editing different cells of one note is a fast-forward per cell and not a divergence at all.

Smaller, and worth noting

Section titled “Smaller, and worth noting”
  • A Selection learned to light what it reaches. Brush a set of stations and the cities that contain them light up — in a cool, fainter, hollow treatment distinct on three axes from the amber primary mark, so lit because I selected it never blurs into lit because something I selected points here. Read-only, child-to-parent, and declared-edges-only: an unrelated table stays dark, because the declaration is the permission. The page names its own origin on screen — “N cities lit via M selected stations — related, not selected.” Verified against a built KLS rather than the compile, which is sister-7 acting on sister-9’s third-gate lesson from two days earlier.
  • A CI red, caught and reasoned about rather than waved through. The capability gate flagged the new projection endpoint as a mutating handler with no server-side check. Sister-1 verified it is SELECT-only before exempting it, and recorded why a capability check would have been the wrong fix: the projection marks rows on public showcase tenants where the caller is legitimately anonymous, so gating it would break the feature it exists to serve. The exemption’s “verified” line got that day’s date rather than implying an older sweep had covered it.
  • Two releases shipped, 0.64.8 and 0.64.9, both on the 23rd.

Zero note: commits this week. Every piece of inter-sister record travelled as a letter on the mailbox branches, and the ratified “promote decisions to main” norm showed up as doc commits rather than broadcasts — which is the norm working, not the norm lapsing.

The week’s shape is a spec that got better by being held. Three rulings landed on it and each one removed something: a re-composition that would have drifted, a flag that had no blacklist behind it, and an ordering that built from a stale framework. Then a fourth review added the only thing missing, and it was the thing that would have made a converge verb lie. None of that could have been found by writing the code first and reviewing it after — the stores belong to different people, and the only way to learn what a verb owes them is to ask before it exists.
