Why it is shaped this way

The companion to the roadmap. That one says where this is going; this one says why it is built the way it is, and then gives an honest inventory of what exists — including the parts that are only specified, and the parts that are wrong today.

Five design decisions do most of the work. They were not taken for elegance: each is a response to a way that analytical software usually fails.

01The problem

The gap is never “what happened”. It is “why, and what do I do”.

Organisations sit on data they do not understand. A hospital's supply chain leaks revenue. A freight forwarder loses sight of a shipment between checkpoints. A law firm bills hours it cannot trace. The data exists. The questions exist.

What is missing is a systematic way to get from “something looks wrong” to “here is why, and here is what to do about it.”

Dashboards do not close that gap; they widen it. A dashboard answers one question and raises three, and the three are asked of a person who then writes a query by hand. The knowledge earned that way lives in that person, and leaves with them. Every decision below follows from refusing that outcome.

02The reasoning

Five decisions, each answering a specific failure

DeclareA question is a declaration, not code

You write what should be detected — in a short, reviewable file. The engine compiles it to SQL and runs it. Fails otherwise: hand-written queries nobody can audit, re-derived per customer.

EscalateSignal → thesis → verdict

An observation, then is this systematic?, then why — ending in a recommendation with its evidence attached. Fails otherwise: alerts that say a number is red and nothing else.

AttributeExpert knowledge is a first-class citizen

What the veteran knows — the workaround, the system quirk, the event that explains the data — is captured with its author, its scope and its why. Fails otherwise: institutional memory leaves with the person.

ContainOne portable store, carrying its own framework

Every build produces a single file holding the data, the findings and the framework that produced them. Freeze it, send it, open it years later. Fails otherwise: a result nobody can reproduce.

Stay putThe data does not have to move

Each customer is a sealed cell, and the same product runs on a laptop, behind a private tunnel, or in the cloud — your data, your machine, their browser. Fails otherwise: sensitive data uploaded because the tool demanded it.

03The commercial shape

Packs make expertise reusable — and we say the awkward part out loud

A domain pack is a whole analytical framework for a field: the data contract, the extractors, twenty-odd signals, theses, and a catalogue of expert knowledge. Ten exist — hospital material flow, workforce, agriculture, air quality, the electricity grid, freshwater, wine, legal, logistics, alpine operations. Installing one gives a new customer a working framework on day one.

And then the honest part, which is on our public site in these words: the pack is a head start; the analysis is the work. Fitting the framework to this customer's data, calibrating the signals, capturing what their people know — that is the first month, and it is analytical work, not configuration.

We lead with that because it is true, and because the flexibility that makes it necessary is exactly what a dashboard product cannot offer. It also sets an honest expectation about where the effort goes, before anyone signs anything.

05SpecifiedDesigned, not built

What is written down but not yet running

Each of these has a specification, an author and an argument behind it. None of them has code. They are listed because a roadmap you cannot audit is a wish list:

  • The register. A machine-readable statement of which customers an installation serves. Today that is a disk scan plus knowledge in one person's head.
  • Extending a store in place. Answer a new question against an existing knowledge store without re-running the customer's pipeline — which is also the only way it can work in the cloud, where the raw data never goes.
  • The outward contract, served. One contract, many transports — including MCP, so an AI agent is a first-class caller rather than a bolt-on. The protocol decision is made; nothing is served yet.
  • Processors beyond SQL. Heavier analysis — source apportionment on air quality is the first candidate — placed on top of the settled data like inserts on a recording, each emitting its own dataset and never mutating what came before.

The line between this slide and the last one is the whole reason both exist. Ambiguity about which side something sits on is how technical roadmaps mislead people.

06Wrong right nowStated deliberately

What we know is broken, this week

  • 29 findings from our own adversarial review — 7 critical, 15 of them security — with a scheduled lockout at the end of September and the highest-value mitigation already going in. It was postponed on the reasoning that there are no customers and therefore no customer data at stake.
  • Parts of the public documentation are older than the system. One page still describes a cloud design as forthcoming. It shipped. A stranger reading us today meets a version of jinflow that no longer exists.
  • Some shipped paths have never been run end to end against the real thing — they typecheck, they are plausible, and that is not the same as verified. Fixing that is a discipline change, not a feature.
  • The probe that exists to detect a bad deployment was itself blind for weeks — it reported a placeholder instead of the running version. Found and fixed this week; it goes live on the next deploy.

This slide is here on purpose. Every product has one; most do not publish it, and the ones that cannot write it are the ones to worry about.

07Where that leaves us

A working system, an honest gap, and a plan with dates

jinflow is seven months old, in production, serving eleven tenants, released twenty-one times in the last thirty days — and has no customers. Everything above is either how that gets closed or why it was worth building first.

What the reasoning buys, if it holds: a new question is a declaration rather than a project; the answer arrives with its provenance attached; the knowledge stays with the organisation rather than the analyst; and the data never has to leave the building for any of it.

The plan for the next sixteen weeks is a separate document, with phases, dates, confidence markers and a done-when for each — and it names the one thing that depends on somebody who is not us: a first design partner with a real dataset and a real question.
